## Cluster: Membrane Transport

[q tier=R]
Define osmosis as introduced in this lecture.
[/q]

[a]
Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration, driven by the concentration gradient.
[/a]

[q tier=U]
Explain why facilitated diffusion requires a protein channel but does not require ATP.
[/q]

[a]
Facilitated diffusion moves substances down their concentration gradient (high to low), which is thermodynamically spontaneous and requires no energy input. The protein channel simply provides a hydrophilic pathway through the hydrophobic lipid bilayer, lowering the activation energy for transport without consuming ATP.
[/a]

[q tier=A]
Predict what happens to a red blood cell placed in distilled water.
[hint] A strong answer must address tonicity, net water movement direction, and the physical consequence for cell volume. [/hint]
[/q]

[a]
Distilled water is hypotonic relative to the cell's cytoplasm. Water moves into the cell by osmosis down its concentration gradient. The cell swells and, if water influx continues, undergoes crenation in reverse — it lyses (bursts) due to the inability of the plasma membrane to withstand the increased turgor pressure.
[/a]

## Cluster: Electrochemical Gradients

[q tier=R]
What is the resting membrane potential of a typical neuron, and which ion is primarily responsible for it?
[/q]

[a]
The resting membrane potential is approximately −70 mV (inside negative relative to outside). It is primarily maintained by the differential permeability to K⁺ and the continuous activity of the sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase).
[/a]

[q tier=U]
Describe how the sodium-potassium pump contributes to both the chemical and electrical gradients across the membrane.
[/q]

[a]
The pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle, consuming one ATP. This creates a chemical gradient (high Na⁺ outside, high K⁺ inside) and contributes to the electrical gradient because it is electrogenic — it expels more positive charges than it imports, making the inside more negative.
[/a]

[q tier=A]
A drug blocks all voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in a neuron. Predict the effect on action potential generation.
[hint] Must address the role of Na⁺ influx in depolarisation and what happens when it is prevented. [/hint]
[/q]

[a]
Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are responsible for the rapid depolarisation phase of the action potential. Blocking them prevents the large inward Na⁺ current needed to bring the membrane to threshold and beyond. Without depolarisation, no action potential is generated. The neuron remains at or near its resting membrane potential and cannot propagate a signal — analogous to the mechanism of local anaesthetics like lidocaine.
[/a]

## Synthesis Questions

[q tier=A]
Connect osmosis across the plasma membrane to the sodium-potassium pump's role in maintaining cell volume over time.
[hint] Must address both passive (osmosis) and active (pump) mechanisms and how they interact to prevent continuous water influx. [/hint]
[/q]

[a]
If Na⁺ were allowed to accumulate inside the cell, the increased intracellular solute concentration would draw water in by osmosis, causing the cell to swell. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump continuously expels Na⁺, keeping intracellular Na⁺ low. This reduces the osmotic driving force for water entry and maintains cell volume homeostasis. The pump's electrogenic activity also sustains the membrane potential needed for downstream signalling. Osmosis is thus the passive threat; the pump is the active defence against volume dysregulation.
[/a]
